Teaching Statement

My teaching is grounded in curiosity, clarity, and care. Whether in theory, analysis, or composition, I want students to experience music as something that belongs to them: a medium through which to think, to make, and to connect. Teaching, for me, is a creative act—an extension of the same processes of exploration and reflection that animate my composition and research. I aim to model a balance of intellectual rigor and imaginative freedom, encouraging students to approach music as an integral part of the wider tapestry of human inquiry: literature, philosophy, history, mathematics, and science.

At Duke I have designed and delivered courses both independently and collaboratively, developed and graded assessments, and led lessons and courses. I have taught I have taught Music 161: Introduction to Music Theory, Music 261L: Theory and Practice of Tonal Music, and Music 273S: Introduction to Electronic Music Composition as instructor of record, contributed significant guest lectures to Nineteenth-Century Music and Musical Shakespeare, and twice assisted in the Introduction to Electronic Music course.

I use backward course design, beginning with learning goals and objectives, then planning meaningful assessments and activities that support them. I regard each syllabus I have written as a living document, revised through reflection, classroom experience, and peer observation. I emphasize transparency and accessibility—clear expectations, inclusive content, and diverse learning resources. For example, in Music 161 I introduced scaffolding strategies for new learners and redesigned assessments to make the rationale for each assignment explicit. For my own syllabus, Songs of the Earth: Music in a Time of Climate Crisis, I combined creative and critical approaches to the climate crisis but upon reflection and discussion with a senior colleague diversified the content covered to improve representation across the course.

Active learning underpins my teaching. In Music 261L I used “one-minute papers” to identify shared challenges and shape future lessons. In Music 161 an anonymous online portal allowed students to submit questions before the midterm, while a collaborative playlist assignment encouraged stylistic diversity and peer discussion around identity and genre. In a guest lecture on Schumann’s Dichterliebe, students drew poems from a “lucky dip,” imagined their own settings, and then compared them to Schumann’s—an approach blending history, imagination, and collective reflection.

I see feedback as central to learning, not an afterthought, balancing formative and summative assessment, peer review, drafts, and dialogue to scaffold growth. My feedback is timely, selective, and specific. Anonymous course evaluations note that I am “very engaging and ready to work with” students and that “the way Chris helped us practice for specific skills was exceptional.”

Teaching is, finally, a reciprocal act. My students’ development and my own are intertwined; reflection, feedback, and professional learning continually reshape my practice. I aim to create classrooms where curiosity, creativity, and intellectual openness thrive—values that produce and contribute to a vibrant, collaborative musical community.

Creative Portfolio

I. Acoustic Works

1. Beyond what is visible, is what is

~4.5 mins — Orchestra

Score (PDF) · Recording

This work emerges from an ongoing textual experiment: a digital corpus of ‘visionary and prophetic’ texts spanning millennia and cultures — from the Babylonian Astronomical Diaries and the Book of Traversing Eternity to Nostradamus and Blake. I trained a rudimentary large language model (LLM) on this corpus to become a kind of Post-Information-Age oracle, exploring how a definitionally ‘superhuman’ system might refract the profoundly human impulse toward prophecy. Part of a larger chresmology (a collection of prophecies) for orchestra, each movement responds to the model’s first utterances. This movement further excavates the cultural past through the collision of heavily transformed musical fragments: Barber, Messiaen, Mahler, and even transcriptions of wind chimes haunt its surface.

2. The true ceremony of the sun: String Quartet no. 1

~16 mins — String Quartet

Score (PDF) · Recording

The opening movement of this quartet examines the tension between voice-leading strategies and harmonic result, using small, continuous motions between pitch classes to create richly variegated harmonic surfaces. The work thus serves as an early demonstration of the compositional ideas later formalised in my theoretical research on harmonic qualia and the DFT. The piece unfolds as a series of cycles within cycles. The first and last movements are more connected to one another than the middle, and all three movements unfold in three parts, so that we are perpetually in motion, always returning to the beginning again.

3. Ave Regina caelorum

~7 mins — SSAATTBB vocal ensemble

Score (PDF)

In addition to a Marian antiphon, at the very end of this piece I have inserted a fragment of a very different, though thematically related, text, by G. K. Chesterton (1874–1936). For me, it serves to suddenly bring the Latin, and so the antiquated Marian adoration, to life in an immediate and palpable way, before the final farewell. As at a few points in the piece, this is a final glimmer of intensity before the close of day — the Compline (or ‘Night Prayer’) — from which the antiphon comes.

II. Electronic / Algorithmic Works

1. Trinomial Overshot 11

~3 mins — Fixed media / electronic · no score

Listen on SoundCloud

Trinomial Overshot 11 seeks to forge a new and distinct relationship to “the grid” and to periodicity itself. Inspired by the unusual weaving patterns devised by American weaver Ada Dietz, I wrote an algorithm combining OpenMusic and Common Lisp that generates and allows me to audition stochastic, hierarchical patterns of irregular pulse duration. This piece is built entirely around five such rhythms, each of eleven pulses, colliding with additional aleatoric elements. The title, Trinomial Overshot 11, refers to Ada Dietz’s own beautiful tapestry of the same name.

III. Film / Applied Music

1. Prometheus opening credits (re-score)

~5 mins — Film score · video only

This video presents my original re-scoring of the opening sequence from Prometheus, performed by the ORT – Orchestra della Toscana under the direction of Hollywood conductor Pete Anthony, at the Teatro Verdi in Florence. For this score, I received the Best Orchestration award in the Chigiana Film Scoring Program, a course selected and taught by members of the USC Thornton Screen Scoring faculty — including Patrick Kirst and Lawrence Shragge — one of the world’s leading programs for composition in visual media.

For rights reasons, excerpts from the original film appear only briefly in this footage.
